The Hurdle Is 4.6 Percent. The Asset Is Bitcoin.

How a chain of rolling, zeroed-out GRATs converts Bitcoin's violent drawdowns into systematic, gift-tax-free transfers — compounding appreciation out of the taxable estate two years at a time.

The federal wealth-transfer system prices every gift against one number: the §7520 rate — 4.6% as of April 2026, reset monthly by the IRS (the simulator below always uses the actual rate for each funding month). Whatever a properly built trust earns above that hurdle during its term passes to the next generation free of gift and estate tax. Bitcoin has cleared that hurdle in nearly every two-year window of its existence — and in the windows where it has not, the structure described below loses nothing. No gift. No exemption consumed. The coins simply come home and try again at a lower price.

That asymmetry is the whole argument. The rest of this article is the machinery.

The Problem Conventional Planning Gets Wrong

The client this strategy serves already exists in large numbers: the long-term holder with a low-basis position now worth millions. Selling triggers a punishing capital gains bill. Holding until death earns the heirs a basis step-up under I.R.C. §1014 — solving the income tax problem — but leaves the entire appreciated position inside the gross estate, exposed to a 40% federal estate tax above the exemption — $15 million per person in 2026, as set by the 2025 tax legislation. The holder is locked in both directions.

Bitcoin compounds the difficulty because it is a bearer asset. Whoever controls the private keys controls the property. A balance on an exchange is not Bitcoin; it is a contractual claim against a custodian, and that claim behaves badly at exactly the moments estate planning exists for — platforms freeze accounts at notice of death, generally will not retitle accounts to a trust, and reserve the right to restrict access in their terms of service.

Conventional planners mishandle this asset in two predictable ways. Some treat the volatility as disqualifying and steer the client away entirely. Others accept the asset but wrap it — in a custodial account or a spot ETF — severing the client from the property itself. Both responses miss the central point: inside the right structure, the volatility is not the obstacle. It is the input.

The Instrument: A Zeroed-Out Two-Year GRAT

A grantor retained annuity trust is an irrevocable trust into which the grantor transfers assets while retaining a fixed annuity for a set term. Under I.R.C. §2702(b), that retained annuity is a qualified interest, and its present value — discounted at the §7520 rate — is subtracted from the transfer when computing the taxable gift. Following Walton v. C.I.R., 115 T.C. 589 (2000), and the IRS's acquiescence in Notice 2003-72, the annuity can be set high enough that the computed gift is zero. This is the zeroed-out, or Walton, GRAT.

The deal it strikes is simple. If the trust assets outperform the §7520 rate over the term, everything above the hurdle passes to the remainder beneficiaries free of gift and estate tax. If they underperform, the annuity simply returns the principal to the grantor — no transfer occurs, but also no loss: no gift was made and no lifetime exemption was used.

The mechanics that matter are in the regulations. The annuity must actually be paid, annually, from income or principal (Treas. Reg. §25.2702-3(b)(1)); the trust cannot satisfy it with a note (Treas. Reg. §25.2702-3(d)(6)(i)); and payments may escalate up to 20% per year (Treas. Reg. §25.2702-3(b)(1)(ii)). Those rules are why long-term GRATs punish volatile assets: in a year when the asset falls 60% — as Bitcoin did in 2014, 2018, and 2022 — the trustee must still pay the fixed annuity, liquidating corpus at the bottom. A two-year term confines each bet to a discrete window and never asks the trust to survive a full cycle.

The Engine: Rolling the Chain

A rolling GRAT is a series of short-term zeroed-out GRATs run as a continuous chain. Each annuity payment the grantor receives — in kind, in Bitcoin — is immediately re-contributed to a new two-year GRAT, zeroed out at that day's price and that month's §7520 rate. Because each GRAT is a grantor trust, transfers between the grantor and the trust are non-recognition events under Rev. Rul. 85-13: the coins move in and out with no taxable sale, and basis carries over.

Now consider what failure means inside the chain. If Bitcoin falls during a term, the trust's assets are consumed by the annuity and return to the grantor. Nothing transferred — and nothing was spent. The grantor re-funds the next GRAT at the depressed price, which resets the hurdle from the new, lower base. When the asset recovers, the successor GRAT captures the entire recovery above 4.6% as a tax-free remainder. The drawdown did not break the strategy; it repositioned it.

Every crash is a re-entry point for the next GRAT in the chain.

Successful remainders distribute in kind to an irrevocable children's trust, where they compound outside the grantor's estate — while the grantor, taxed personally on all trust income under the grantor trust rules, keeps depleting the taxable estate with every tax payment (a benefit the IRS itself confirmed is not a gift, Rev. Rul. 2004-64). Contrast the installment sale to a defective grantor trust: there the note is fixed at the original price, a crash leaves the trust underwater, and no reset mechanism exists. The rolling GRAT cannot fail catastrophically. It can only fail gracefully, and graceful failure is a re-entry.

Why Bitcoin: Volatility as the Feature

The textbook GRAT asset is a pre-IPO interest or property approaching a known liquidity event — concentrated, predictable, and non-repeating. Once the company goes public, there is nothing to roll into. Bitcoin is the opposite case: its supply issuance halves on a four-year schedule embedded in the protocol, and every cycle to date has produced both a deep drawdown and a recovery that dwarfs any plausible §7520 hurdle.

The crash amplified the strategy.

Operational Reality: Keys, Vaults, and the 105-Day Lever

In-kind annuities and the grace window

Every payment in the chain should move in Bitcoin, not dollars. An in-kind annuity is valued at fair market value on the date of distribution, triggers no recognition under Rev. Rul. 85-13, and never forces the trustee to sell into a drawdown. Consistent use of a recognized pricing source — for example, the CME CF Bitcoin Reference Rate at a fixed daily time — keeps each valuation defensible.

The regulations then hand the trustee a free option most planners never use: Treas. Reg. §25.2702-3(b)(4) permits each annuity payment to be made up to 105 days after its due date. Within that window, paying on a local price high means fewer coins leave the trust to satisfy the fixed dollar obligation. In historical backtests this single lever — no structural change, no cost — improved aggregate transfers across a rolling chain by roughly 11%.

One seed, thirty vaults

Each GRAT in the chain is a separate irrevocable trust and needs its own titled custody: a multisignature vault owned by that GRAT (“2026 GRAT No. 1 Vault”), typically a 2-of-3 quorum split among the trustee, an independent co-signer, and a collaborative custody provider that holds one key but can never move funds unilaterally. Funding, each annuity, and the remainder distribution are all on-chain transactions — publicly timestamped, with the transaction ID serving as a transfer record more auditable than any brokerage letter.

The obvious objection — that a thirty-GRAT chain implies an unmanageable drawer of hardware — misunderstands how Bitcoin keys work. Under BIP-32 hierarchical deterministic derivation, a single seed produces an effectively unlimited tree of cryptographically independent child keys, one derivation path per GRAT. Thirty vaults require three hardware devices — one per signer — plus a descriptor registry mapping each trust to its path and quorum. A compromised key at one path does not propagate to the others, and a replacement device initialized from the same seed reproduces every vault.

It is worth saying plainly, because the intuition runs the other way: for the rolling structure, this is operationally easier than the conventional alternative. Run the same chain with a spot ETF in brokerage accounts and every new GRAT requires opening a new account at the brokerage in the name of a new trust — a fresh application, entity documentation, KYC review, and signature authority setup, typically weeks per entity, multiplied across every link in the chain. In the multisignature architecture, a new GRAT is a derivation step: the same three signing devices, the next account index on the tree, a new vault descriptor titled to the new trust, recorded in the registry — minutes of work, no new institution, no new hardware, no new credentials. The trustee's signing ceremony is identical for vault one and vault thirty. The structure that looks exotic scales; the structure that looks familiar does not.

Why custodial and ETF wrappers break it

None of this can be executed on an exchange. A custodial balance is a claim, not property; the trustee cannot exercise dominion and control over an IOU, most platforms will not retitle accounts to a trust entity, and accounts freeze at notice of death — mid-term, with annuities still owed. A spot Bitcoin ETF works mechanically for the two-year term but fails the remainder, which is the entire point of the exercise: the children's trust holds for decades, compounding an expense ratio and a sponsor-plus-custodian counterparty stack, and because retail shares cannot be redeemed in kind, eventually converting to actual Bitcoin means a sale — precisely the recognition event the grantor trust architecture exists to avoid. Self-custodied coins move from grantor vault to GRAT vault to children's trust vault with no sale at any step.

The Honest Risks

Mortality. If the grantor dies during a term, I.R.C. §2036(a) pulls GRAT assets back into the gross estate — a risk the Ninth Circuit confirmed is real in Badgley v. United States, 957 F.3d 969 (9th Cir. 2020). Two-year terms keep each exposure window short, but the risk cannot be engineered to zero; a life insurance hedge in an irrevocable trust is the standard complement.

Legislation. Recurring proposals — including in recent administration budget requests — would impose a ten-year minimum GRAT term and ban zeroed-out structures. Nothing has been enacted as of this writing, but either change would break new chains. The window is open, not guaranteed.

GST and the ETIP. During each GRAT term the trust sits inside an estate tax inclusion period under I.R.C. §2642(f): no generation-skipping exemption allocation is effective until the term ends, and allocation at that point is measured against the appreciated value — ruinously expensive for a GRAT that worked. The clean architecture routes GRAT remainders to a children's trust and runs dynasty-level GST planning on a separate, directly exemption-funded track.

The gift is near zero, not zero. Because a zeroed-out annuity rate necessarily exceeds the §7520 rate, the exhaustion test of Treas. Reg. §25.7520-3(b)(2) treats the corpus as potentially depleted before the final payment, leaving a small but nonzero reportable gift. The planner should model the economic remainder — what actually passes at real prices — rather than rely on the actuarial one.

Suitability. This is not conservative planning. It is a high-conviction structure for clients above the exemption who hold Bitcoin they are willing to commit to irrevocable trusts, who accept operational complexity, and whose trustees can actually run multisig signing ceremonies and in-kind distributions — or can delegate to someone who can, under fiduciary oversight the trust instrument expressly authorizes. A trustee who cannot operate the custody is a trustee who cannot administer the trust.

One honest paragraph on what produces the cycle, because the strategy's premise leans on it. The mechanical story is supply — every 210,000 blocks the new-coin subsidy halves — but that flow has collapsed from roughly 12.5% of supply per year in the first epoch to 0.83% today, headed for ~0.4% after 2028; published event studies find halving effects that shrink with each epoch; and Bitcoin's long-run correlation with global money supply runs near 0.9, so the halving clock and the liquidity cycle are confounded at three observations. The 2024–2026 cycle was the live experiment: ETF demand ran roughly ten times daily mined supply and prominent institutional voices declared the four-year cycle dead — yet the market topped 534 days after the halving (beside 526 and 548 in the prior two cycles), then fell by half. The period held; the amplitude decayed. Miner economics point the same direction: …. But that swing compresses every epoch, miner distribution actually clusters at capitulation bottoms rather than tops, and daily issuance is now roughly a tenth of a percent of spot volume. What survives is less a supply shock than a calendar the market keeps honoring — which is exactly the signature in the figure above: stable period, decaying amplitude.

So does the data still support the strategy? The bar it must clear is not "repeat 2017." A rolling GRAT needs the median two-year window to beat roughly a ten percent cumulative hurdle, with the failures costing nothing. That weaker condition has held in every epoch so far — but the slack is shrinking, and the unconditional success rate has slid from the high eighties to the mid sixties. Methodology & Engineering Notes

  • Engine. The simulator is a line-for-line JavaScript port of a Python research engine (grat_timing.py) covered by 26 unit tests pinned to the worked examples in Planning an Estate: A Guidebook of Principles and Techniques §§9:6, 8:44 — the Walton level-annuity factor (15-yr, $1M at 6% → $102,963), the 20% escalating schedule (2-yr, $1M at 2.8% → $474,316 then $569,179), and the §2036(a) capitalization with its trust-value cap. Python and JavaScript outputs agree to full floating-point precision on identical scenarios.
  • Conservation invariant. Every chain certifies that the BTC funded into GRAT No. 1 equals dynasty remainders + coins still in trust at the data horizon + coins returned to the grantor un-rolled + the gated holding pool + corpus swept to the estate at death — to ~10−13 BTC. The residual is shown live under the summary cards.
  • Rates. Each GRAT is zeroed out at the actual IRS §7520 rate for its funding month (monthly table, 2010–2026, transcribed from the IRS rate tables — verify any month you rely on). The annuity rate of a zeroed-out short GRAT always exceeds the §7520 rate, implicating the exhaustion test of Treas. Reg. §25.7520-3(b)(2); the model reports the economic remainder, and the tiny reportable gift is noted rather than modeled.
  • Data. Daily BTC price, drawdown-from-ATH, MVRV Z-score, Mayer Multiple, realized price, NUPL, Pi-Cycle moving averages, and the long-term-holder cost basis from the NewHedge API, snapshotted at build time. No API calls or credentials exist in this page; the snapshot date is printed at the top of the simulator. The hurdle-clearance figure in the article and all chart annotations are computed client-side from this snapshot — nothing is hand-drawn.
  • Entry signal. A transparent, fixed-weight composite of cycle-position metrics (drawdown 30%, MVRV-Z 20%, Mayer 20%, price/realized 15%, months-since-halving 15%). The weights encode the article's thesis; they are not fitted, and they are deliberately simple enough to audit by eye.
  • Analysis era. There is no universal convention for where Bitcoin backtests begin, but academic work typically starts in 2013 or later, citing pre-2013 exchange data quality and liquidity (Mt. Gox-era records are incomplete and manipulable), and cycle studies anchor on the halving epochs. This page defaults all statistics to the halving era (first halving, Nov 28, 2012 →) — the regime the strategy's thesis depends on — with two alternatives one click away: full history (2010 →, inflates every statistic with returns that were not executable at modeled position sizes) and the custody era (2017 →, when trust-grade multisig custody practically existed). The price chart always shows full history for context; the era governs which start dates enter the statistics.
  • Compression analysis. The diminishing-returns section computes, per halving epoch: forward two-year window multiples against each funding month's compounded §7520 hurdle, a log-log regression of window outcome on entry price, fixed-runway chains seeded on each halving date (the open epoch's chain is right-censored, not failed), NewHedge's 1-year realized volatility, and mature-era hurdle clearance bucketed by entry drawdown. All of it recomputes from the snapshot on every load.
  • Scenario model. The next-decade table is a seeded Monte Carlo (next_cycle_model.py, results in next_cycle_results.json / site/scenarios.js): five deterministic cycle skeletons anchored to the historical leg durations and decay sequences, multiplied by 300 mean-zero log-normal noise paths each, run through the same verified chain engine at a flat 5.0% §7520 (a stated simplification). Scenario parameters are published in full; no probabilities are assigned to scenarios. The cycle and miner claims in this section draw on protocol arithmetic, the NewHedge mining series (Puell Multiple, miner revenue, reserves, outflows), published event studies of halvings, and institutional research on the macro-liquidity correlation; positions on both sides of the "cycle is dead" question are represented as attributed views, not endorsements. The asset-class comparison runs the identical chain over stylized constant-drift paths for equities, a 60/40 portfolio, and gold (asset_comparison.py → compare.js, seeds documented); the conventional-GRAT benchmarks quoted in the prose draw on practitioner Monte Carlo research on diversified-equity rolling GRATs published in Trusts & Estates (2008–2024) and the public record of concentrated-stock GRATs.
  • Honesty constraints. Distributions over ~4 Bitcoin cycles are tiny samples; later start dates are right-censored (fewer generations fit before the data ends); chains compound path-dependence. Every number on this page is a description of history, not a forecast of anything.
